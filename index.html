Annotation

This curriculum map is built on the Applied Computing Study Design (VCAA, 2024).

The map can be read as two parts: unit sequence and problem-solving methodology (PSM). Unit sequence shows a choice architecture: Units 1 and 2 are common, after which students can select Data Analytics or Software Development. The arrows show a typical pathway, but not a prerequisite chain.

The second part is the problem-solving methodology, drawn as a cycle and connected to the units it covers. Its four stages explain why the School-assessed Task (SAT) spans two units.

Reflection

Teaching Reflection and Pedagogical Analysis

How I would teach with the seven resources in this portfolio, and what that design assumes about guidance, technology and time.

Pedagogical approach

My approach to these resources is constructivist, but constructivism alone does not settle the design. Students have to generate design ideas themselves in the lesson. The problem is that generation without a sense of what separates ideas produces cosmetic variation, and the outcome rewards genuine divergence. Dangol and Dasgupta (2023) reviewed forty-eight constructionist data projects with young people. They found that such projects reliably engage students in making, yet often stop short of critical questions about power and interest. Resource 5 is built on exactly that gap. Its case study is correct by the method students were taught, and still indefensible in its effects. The advantage is that students cannot dismiss it on instinct. They have to prove it works before they can argue it is wrong.

However, Hung, Hwang and Huang (2012) pointed out the limitations of project-based learning. Their quasi-experiment added a storytelling structure to ordinary project-based learning. The structured group gained more in problem-solving competence than the conventional group. I take this as evidence that project work needs a device that forces articulation. Resource 6 is that device here, and it costs fifteen minutes rather than a unit. Resource 6 also changes who owns the feedback. Nieminen et al. (2022) argue that feedback research treats agency as an individual, psychological property. They propose locating agency in relationships and materials instead. An exemplar pair does precisely that. Students judge two artefacts against each other before I say anything, so the judgement is theirs to defend.

The sequencing follows from this. Resource 1 moves definitions out of class, so lesson time opens on application. Resource 2 shows six contrasting pairs before any principle is named. Resource 3 comes last, because building before arguing produces justifications written backwards (Papert, 1980).

Technology integration

Zhu and Wang (2024) interviewed K-12 computer science teachers and instructors about their challenges. Students’ technology access was one of the three they named. That finding shaped a deliberate choice: six of my seven resources need no device. Resource 4 is a printed page, Resource 5 and Resource 6 are documents, and Resource 2 is a set of screenshots. Only Resource 3 requires a digital tool. A paper sketch has to be narrated by its author. A narrated design never fails, because the author closes every gap as it opens. A linked prototype can be handed over and left alone. Hesitation then becomes visible, and visible hesitation is the data students record. Mishra and Koehler (2006) treat technology, pedagogy and content as mutually constituting. My guiding principle is more specific: the digital tool enters only where removing it would remove the learning.

Inclusion follows the same logic. The greyscale rule strips the advantage held by students with design experience or better hardware. A supplied starter file removes tool fluency as a precondition for taking part. Resource 1’s video is free, three minutes long, captioned and rewatchable. For students working in an additional language, that is worth more than an adjustment offered afterwards.

Curriculum connection

Unit 3 Outcome 2 asks students to document an analysis and generate design ideas and a preferred design (VCAA, 2024). I watched the VCAA implementation briefing before planning, and it gave me more than a boundary. The briefing requires a folio of design ideas and evaluation criteria. It never says what makes a criterion do any work (VCAA, 2025a). Resources 5 and 6 are designed to address this issue. Grover and Pea (2013) make the general version of this argument. They contend that computational thinking is not absorbed incidentally and needs deliberate instructional attention. Evaluation criteria work the same way. A study design can name a capability without making it teachable, and that gap is the teacher’s to close. Resource 4 carries the reasoning across the assessment task. The School-assessed Task spans Unit 3 Outcome 2 and Unit 4 Outcome 1. A skill shown once in one annotated decision has to survive eight weeks of changing requirements. Pucher and Lehner (2011) reviewed more than five hundred computer science projects. They found that students hit trouble in the early phases, before a teacher notices. A weekly sheet makes those early blanks visible while they are still easy to fix.

Challenges and risks

Pucher and Lehner (2011) also name the risk I find hardest. Evaluating project results is difficult to standardise, and teacher remarks depend heavily on individual judgement. My design raises that exposure, because Resource 6 asks me to separate two complete, correctly termed responses. The counter is partly structural. An exemplar pair gives the class and me one shared anchor instead of my private standard. In addition, moderation with colleagues is what keeps the standard consistent beyond my own judgement.

The second risk is that the design may lead students to learn in order to cope with the assessment. Every resource asks students to name what a decision ruled out, and that demand is assessed. A student who studies Resource 6 closely has been shown what a convincing justification looks like. Shute (2008) concludes that formative feedback works when it is specific, timely and non-evaluative. Attaching marks to Resource 1’s retrieval check would turn a diagnostic into a performance, so it stays ungraded. Resource 4 defends the same way over time. Asking weekly for the rejected alternative, against a changing specification, makes fabrication expensive to sustain.

For the other two smaller risks, I have also developed corresponding solutions. The implementation of Resource 1 relies on the cooperation of students, so I prepared alternative plans in the lesson plan in case of emergencies. Resource 6 is likely to lead to students mechanically imitating it, so both responses use a project unlike any student’s own.

Also, time is the constraint I cannot resolve. Resources 2, 5 and 6 cost roughly forty minutes across two lessons of an already crowded outcome.

Professional learning goals

Two gaps are visible in the design. The first is judging evaluation criteria consistently across a class set of folios. The study design names the capability but does not model it. This is a moderation skill, and moderation is social interaction. Zhu and Wang (2024) found that teachers wanted content and pedagogical knowledge, and also an ongoing learning community. That matches what I need. A single workshop will not calibrate a judgement I have to make twenty-five times. Digital Learning and Teaching Victoria is the subject network where such calibration is argued rather than received. VCAA’s School-assessed Task advice and regional moderation meetings are the formal sources.

The second gap is about teaching practice. I do not yet know how to stop a student rescuing their own prototype mid-test. Planning cannot supply that. Watching an experienced teacher and debriefing afterwards can. Nieminen et al. (2022) argue that agency is relational. The same holds for my own development. Both goals depend on comparing my judgements with those of other practitioners repeatedly.

For teachers

Public-Facing Contribution

A short post for other teachers of senior digital technologies, on where design thinking belongs in VCE Applied Computing.

The post

Design thinking is already in your study design

Kuilin (Colin) Chen

There is a steady push to add design thinking to Applied Computing as a separate framework. I think that would be a mistake, and an unnecessary one. Ideation techniques, design tools, criteria for evaluating design ideas and the characteristics of user experience are all named in Units 3 and 4. A second framework gives students two vocabularies and fluency in neither.

What is missing is the part design thinking exists for: contact with a user who is not the designer (Brown, 2008; Razzouk & Shute, 2012). The 2025 examination report shows the cost. On user-centred design, “many responses included that user-centred design aims to meet the needs of the users without mentioning user involvement in the design process.”

Three steps to bridge the gap here:

Make divergence structural. Two design ideas must differ in where the information lives or how the user reaches it, not in colour or arrangement.

Put a user in the room before the design is fixed. Three classmates, one task sentence read aloud, no explaining. Record where they hesitate.

Make the criterion discriminate. A criterion both designs pass is doing no work.

Design thinking is not a unit. It is what the folio should already have been.
